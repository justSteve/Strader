# 04 · Volume Profile — Where the Business Was Done (and Where It Wasn't)

*Foundation series, document 4 of 8. Rests on: [02 · Volume](02-volume.md)
(acceptance/rejection, the "at price" lens) and [03 · Market Profile](03-market-profile.md).*

---

## The one idea

Take the Market Profile picture and swap the counting unit: instead of *time at
price* (half-hour letters), count **contracts traded at each price**. The result —
a sideways histogram called the **Volume Profile** — is the definitive record of
where the auction's business actually happened. Its peaks show where the market
agreed; its valleys show where it refused. The valleys are the treasure: they are
the structure that the entire zone-trading method we model is built on.

## The shape and its two features

```
6260  ▓▓
6258  ▓▓▓▓▓
6256  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓       ← High-Volume Node (HVN)
6254  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
6252  ▓▓▓
6250  ▓                  ← Low-Volume Node (LVN)
6248  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
6246  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓         ← another HVN
6244  ▓▓▓▓
```

**High-Volume Node (HVN)** — a shelf where heavy business was done. In auction
terms: a price the market *accepted*, hard. Both sides transacted in size; positions
live there; a natural population of traders is willing to do business there again.
When price returns to an HVN it tends to **slow down and get sticky** — the shelf
absorbs movement the way a crowded marketplace absorbs a running man.

**Low-Volume Node (LVN)** — a valley where almost nothing traded. This is document
02's "hole" with its proper name now attached. An LVN is not an accident: it is the
scar of a **fast, one-sided departure**. At some moment, one side panicked or
pounced, price fled through those levels so quickly that no business got done, and
the profile never healed. Nobody agreed to trade there. Ever.

## Why an LVN is a decision point, not a resting place

When price *returns* to an LVN later, something has to give, because there's no
population of agreeable traders there to make it sticky. Two outcomes dominate:

1. **Sharp rejection** — the same imbalance that carved the hole shows up again and
   price bounces away fast, or
2. **Fast acceptance-through** — price slices through the thin zone quickly (there's
   nothing there to slow it) and travels to the next HVN shelf.

What an LVN almost never does is host quiet, two-sided rotation. It forces a
decision. That's why the return to an LVN is such a high-quality *moment to pay
attention* — the market must show its hand there, and it usually shows it quickly.

## This is Carmine's whole method, in the vocabulary you now have

Carmine Rosato — the order-flow educator whose approach we model — trades almost
nothing but this structure. His method, restated with the concepts of documents
01–04:

1. A fast, one-sided move (an auction *imbalance*) leaves an LVN behind.
2. He waits — no prediction — for price to **return** to that LVN.
3. At the return, he reads the trade-by-trade behavior (that's document 05, next)
   to judge which of the two outcomes is unfolding: rejection or slice-through.
4. He positions with the answer, with a stop just beyond the zone, because the
   zone's whole nature is "decides quickly."

One more naming note, so other people's material doesn't confuse you: **supply and
demand zones, order blocks, fair-value gaps, "smart money concepts"** — these are
four schools' names for substantially the same scar the same event leaves. A fast
departure creates a thin zone; the schools differ in how they draw the box and the
folklore they attach. We treat LVN as the honest, mechanical name (it's literally
countable from trades) and translate the others onto it.

## The reference levels this lens gives you

Like Market Profile, Volume Profile produces levels that persist:

- **Volume POC** — the highest-volume price of the session (the profile's biggest
  shelf). Usually close to the TPO POC of document 03; when they differ, trust
  the volume version.
- **Naked volume POC** — same "unfinished business" magnet idea as document 03's
  naked POC, measured in volume.
- **Composite profiles** — stack several days (or weeks) of volume into one
  histogram to see the *durable* shelves and holes. Session profiles answer
  "where is today's business?"; composites answer "where does this market
  historically agree and refuse?" Both matter: today's LVN is sharp but young;
  a composite LVN is old but proven.

## How documents 03 and 04 divide the labor

They're two lenses on the same auction (time vs contracts), and they're used
differently in practice:

| | Market Profile (time) | Volume Profile (contracts) |
|---|---|---|
| Best at | Classifying the DAY — balance/imbalance, day type, developing mode | Marking the LEVELS — shelves and holes that will still matter tomorrow |
| Question | "What kind of auction is today?" | "Where exactly will the auction have to make decisions?" |
| Feeds | Go/no-go on the butterfly; trade-day danger call | The map of levels the drills and Carmine-style entries anchor to |

Together they hand you a map: *what kind of day this is* and *where the
decision-forcing prices sit*. What neither can do is tell you what's happening AT
one of those prices in the moment price arrives. For that you need the
trade-by-trade view.
